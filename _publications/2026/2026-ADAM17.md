abstract: >-
  Biliary tract cancer (BTC) lacks effective, broad-spectrum therapeutic targets. To overcome the limitations of conventional target discovery, we developed ACT-Target, a modality-aware computational agent, to systematically identify therapeutic candidates and preclinically validate ADAM17-directed antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells.
We constructed the ACT-Target framework by integrating multi-omics evidence, surface-antigen gating, safety profiling, and structural developability to prioritize therapeutic candidates. Following the omics-driven nomination of ADAM17, we assessed its clinical expression via immunohistochemistry in BTC tissues. Novel murine anti-human ADAM17 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) were generated, characterized, and engineered into MMAE-conjugated ADCs and CAR-T cells to evaluate specific cytotoxicity in vitro and anti-tumor efficacy in vivo.
Time-split benchmarking demonstrated that ACT-Target prospectively recovered 12 of 18 future solid-tumor ADC/CAR-T targets without introducing unsafe false positives. Application to a tumor-only multi-omics cohort prioritized ADAM17 as the top-ranked candidate due to its balanced omics support, surface accessibility, and dual-modality compatibility. Histological analysis confirmed significant ADAM17 upregulation in BTC tissues compared to normal controls. Three lead mAbs exhibited sub-nanomolar affinities and strict target specificity without cross-reactivity. The ADAM17-ADCs and CAR-T cells induced potent, specific in vitro cytotoxicity against BTC cells and profoundly inhibited in vivo tumor growth while demonstrating preclinical tolerability. 
Integrating advanced computational target discovery with targeted immunotherapeutics establishes ADAM17 as a highly prevalent and actionable target in BTC. The ADAM17-ADCs and CAR-T cells exhibit robust preclinical efficacy and a potentially manageable safety profile, offering a versatile dual-modality strategy that warrants further translational evaluation for advanced disease.
